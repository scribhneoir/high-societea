The gala takes place over **nine rounds**. Every third round — the third, the
sixth, and the ninth — a dance will occur. Round turns are played in
clockwise order, beginning to the left of the Host.

<figure class="epigraph">
<blockquote><p>For what do we live, but to make sport for our neighbours, and laugh at them in our turn?</p></blockquote>
<figcaption>— Mr. Bennet, <cite>Pride and Prejudice</cite>, ch. 57</figcaption>
</figure>

# On the Turn

A turn is made of two parts: a character's engagement, and their action.

A character's engagement is the company they keep — a conversation sought out, a
dance joined, or the freedom of the room to themselves. A character who was
drawn into another's conversation earlier in the round has spent their
engagement there already, but still takes an action of their own when their turn
comes around.

A character's action is the one thing they do with that company. They may:

- Attack another character
- Ask another character to dance
- Ask for another character's hand in marriage
- Sell a secret
- Explore the gala, if they are not otherwise engaged
- Speak, and nothing more

# On the Art of Conversation

Being engaged in a conversation takes up a character's engagement portion of the
turn. If a character wishes to speak with someone who is already engaged in
conversation, they may interrupt or tear them away from the initial
conversation. Doing so is rarely well received, and any player who witnesses
it may call Faux Pas upon them for it.

# On Dancing

## Asking Someone to Dance

During the two rounds before a dance, a character may ask another character
to dance. If they are accepted, both characters are engaged to dance during the
next dancing round. If they are rejected, the asking character loses a
reputation point, though they may roll below their own Charm to have the
refusal pass unremarked and lose nothing by it.

## Dancing

During the dancing round, both partners are engaged in conversation with each
other.

At the end of their collective turn, both dancers must make a roll below their
own Accomplishment score. If a dancer is accomplished in the dancing skill,
they may roll with advantage.

If both players succeed, each gains two reputation points. If both fail,
each player loses one reputation point. If one succeeds and the other does
not, the dance passes without remark, and neither gains nor loses.

# On Proposals of Marriage

A character may ask for the hand of another, provided **the two have partnered a
dance at some point in the evening**. An offer to a guest one has never stood up
with is no offer at all, and the room would think very little of it.

If the proposal is accepted, each partner gains three reputation points and the
two are engaged to be married. If it is refused, the asking character loses two
reputation points, though they may roll below their own Charm to have the
refusal pass unremarked and lose nothing by it.

A character may be engaged to only one other at a time, and a character already
engaged may not accept a second offer while the first stands.

## Being Engaged

From the moment an engagement is made, the fortunes of the two are tied.
**When one partner gains or loses reputation, the other gains or loses the
same.** Each takes their share out of their own stats, or pays it from their own
purse, as they see fit.

This sharing resolves once and does not run back and forth between them. A loss
suffered by one is felt by the other, and there the matter ends.

An attack is the one exception. It falls upon the stat of the guest attacked and
nowhere else, and their partner feels nothing of it. A couple would be twice as
easy to ruin otherwise.

## Breaking It Off

Either partner may break the engagement on their turn. The tie between them ends
at once, and only then are the costs reckoned: the one who breaks it loses five
reputation points, and the one who is jilted loses three, for the room will talk
about them both. Neither loss is shared, the engagement being over.

# On Exploration

If not otherwise engaged, a player may explore the gala at their leisure. They
may make advantageous discoveries.

Before the evening begins, the Host names the rooms of the house — a
conservatory, a library, a terrace, a card room — and settles what is to be
found in each. A letter left in a writing desk, a coin fallen behind a cushion,
two guests who did not expect to be overheard: these things are placed
deliberately, and they stay where they are put until somebody comes across them.

A house already furnished is set out in [Discoveries](/discoveries).

A character who explores names the room they wander into and rolls below
their own Wit. A quick mind notices what a dull one walks straight past.

Whatever they find, the Host writes upon a slip of paper and hands to them
discreetly. The Host does the same when nothing has been found at all, so that
nobody at the table may tell from a guest's face whether they have returned from
the conservatory with a letter in their pocket or with nothing but their own
company.

# On Combat

## Attacking

The attacking player **rolls above or equal to the attacked player's stat**. If
the roll succeeds, the attacked player loses a point in that stat. If a
player can indicate an aspect of their character that would benefit them in
their action, the Host may grant them advantage. A failed attack costs the
attacker nothing but their action.

**No character may be attacked more than once in a single round.** A guest
already cut down this round is, for the moment, beneath further notice.

## Retort

If an attacked player wishes, they may attempt to retort by **rolling below
their own Wit**. If the roll succeeds, the initial attacker loses two points
in the stat they attacked, and the attacked player loses none. If the roll
fails, the attacked player loses an additional point in that stat.

Successful retort attacks may be retorted. Each answer is a fresh roll below the
retorting character's own Wit, and the exchange ends the moment one of them
falters. A retort is not an attack, and does not count against the round's
reckoning of who has been attacked.

# Faux Pas

<figure class="epigraph">
<blockquote><p>It was badly done, indeed!</p></blockquote>
<figcaption>— Mr. Knightley, <cite>Emma</cite>, vol. III ch. 7</figcaption>
</figure>

If a player takes an action that is not deemed socially permissible by another
player, that other player may call Faux Pas, briefly describing the action they
considered unacceptable. They may not explain why they consider the action
unacceptable. Players may not speak until a verdict is reached. The host counts
down from three and players in agreement with the call raise their hands. If the
majority declares Faux Pas, the accused loses a reputation point. If the
majority votes against, the accuser loses a reputation point.

In the case of a tie, the Host acts as a tie breaker. Faux Pas may be called as
often as the room has cause, by any player and against any other.

Whichever player the room has judged against — accused or accuser alike — may
roll below their own Charm to carry the moment off regardless. On a success the
verdict passes without cost to them, and they lose nothing. A charming guest may
do the unforgivable and be forgiven for it.

# Selling Secrets

<figure class="epigraph">
<blockquote><p>Selfishness must always be forgiven, you know, because there is no hope of a cure.</p></blockquote>
<figcaption>— Mary Crawford, <cite>Mansfield Park</cite>, ch. 7</figcaption>
</figure>

In the case that a character's reputation is running low, that character may
choose to sell any of the secrets in their hand to regain reputation. The
selling player must be engaged in conversation with the players they wish to
sell their secret to.

To sell a secret, a player must make a copy of the card they hold. The point
value of the copy must be one less than the value of the card it was copied
from. Upon giving this copy to another player, the seller may gain
reputation points equal to the value of the card in their own hand.

A secret may be sold as often as its holder likes, but never twice to the same
buyer. They may not sell a secret to a player who already has a copy of it in
their hand.

A player who has been sold a secret may sell it in turn, copying from the card
in their own hand rather than from the original. A scandal worth five in its
first telling is worth four at second hand and three at third, until at last it
dwindles to nothing and is worth no one's trouble to repeat.

## Crafting Rumors

<figure class="epigraph">
<blockquote><p>Seldom, very seldom, does complete truth belong to any human disclosure; seldom can it happen that something is not a little disguised, or a little mistaken.</p></blockquote>
<figcaption>— <cite>Emma</cite>, vol. III ch. 13</figcaption>
</figure>

If a player wishes, they may write down a secret that they do not have in their
hand, setting upon it whatever value they please. Upon receiving a secret card,
a player may accuse it of being a rumor.

**The character the secret concerns declares whether it is true.** They are
under no obligation to be honest, and a guest will frequently find it convenient
to disown a thing that happens to be perfectly true.

- If the secret is declared true, the accuser has impugned an honest telling and
  loses reputation equal to the value on the card.
- If the secret is declared false, the player who sold it loses reputation
  equal to the value they wrote upon it.
- A seller who did not write the card, having been sold it themselves, loses
  nothing. They must say as much, though they need never say who sold it to
  them. The accusation goes no further and no one pays.

Whichever player the matter has gone against — the accused caught out in a
rumor, or the accuser who has impugned a true secret — may roll below their
own Charm. On a success they lose nothing, having brazened the thing out entirely.

# Outbursts

<figure class="epigraph">
<blockquote><p>I will be calm; I will be mistress of myself.</p></blockquote>
<figcaption>— Elinor Dashwood, <cite>Sense and Sensibility</cite>, ch. 48</figcaption>
</figure>

If any one of a player's stats is **reduced to 0**, that player may perform an
outburst. They may choose to reveal any number of secrets that they hold in
their hand to the entire party.

When a player's secret is revealed publicly, they must remove the amount of
reputation points listed on the original copy, plus the number of copies in
circulation, from their stats. All copies of the secret must be discarded
from all player hands.

If another player's stat reaches zero due to an exposed secret, that player may
perform an outburst once the initial outburst concludes.
